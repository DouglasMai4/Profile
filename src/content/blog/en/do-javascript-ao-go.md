# From JavaScript to Go: The Discomfort That Helped Me Grow

For much of my journey in software development, JavaScript was the language I used to build my professional experience. I started with frontend development, gradually explored more of its ecosystem, and eventually began working on the backend as well. JavaScript and TypeScript became familiar tools in my day-to-day work.

Go, on the other hand, was just a name I had heard a few times. I had never worked with it and, to be honest, had never even looked at its syntax.

That changed when a need came up at [Goalfy](https://goalfy.com.br).

## An Unexpected Challenge

At one point, I became responsible for developing a Governance microservice. It was an important part of the architecture, since the other microservices depended on it, in one way or another, to validate user permissions.

When we started discussing which language to use, Go quickly became the team's unanimous choice. The idea of using it actually came from me.

It might seem contradictory to suggest a language you've never worked with, but I saw it as an opportunity. I didn't know Go, but I believed it would be a good challenge and that I could learn a lot along the way.

There's a phrase written on the wall at Goalfy:

**"Growth is uncomfortable."**

At that moment, I decided to take it literally.

## Learning a Language Is About More Than Its Syntax

When I started learning Go, I realized that picking up a new programming language isn't just about figuring out how to write the same things in a different way.

My experience was heavily rooted in the JavaScript and TypeScript ecosystems. Naturally, some of my first attempts at solving problems involved applying concepts and habits I was already familiar with.

But Go made me question some of those choices.

The language has a different philosophy. Its simplicity, its approach to error handling, the way interfaces work, and its emphasis on avoiding unnecessary complexity made me rethink decisions that had previously felt natural.

That's when the learning process became really interesting.

I didn't just want to write Go code that worked. I wanted to understand how to write idiomatic Go, respecting the language's characteristics and making the most of what it offers.

I started realizing that not every abstraction needed to exist, that an external library wasn't always necessary, and that, quite often, the simplest solution was also the most appropriate one.

This changed not only the way I wrote Go, but also how I evaluated my decisions as a developer.

## From Work to Personal Projects

My professional experience with Go sparked a curiosity that went far beyond the initial need.

I started studying more, experimenting with tools, and building small projects to better understand the language and its ecosystem.

Along the way, I began exploring technologies like Chi and SQLC, while also paying more attention to Go's standard library.

That's also how [Kori](https://kori.douglasmaia.dev.br) came to life, my own toolkit for Go, built on top of Chi. It was a way to turn some of what I was learning into something tangible, experiment with ideas, and share a bit of that journey with others.

Projects like this hold a special value for me. They allow me to move beyond simply consuming documentation and examples and take on the challenge of building something, making decisions, and dealing with the problems that come along the way.

## A Change That Went Beyond the Language

Today, Go has a much bigger place in my daily work than I ever imagined when I took on that first challenge.

What's most interesting is that I don't see this change as replacing JavaScript with Go, nor as a competition to decide which language is better. Each has its own place, strengths, and use cases.

What changed was my perspective.

Learning Go pushed me out of some comfort zones, made me question habits I didn't even realize I had, and helped me understand that there are different ways to approach the same problem.

It also reminded me of something I consider important in our profession: we don't need to know everything before taking on a challenge. Sometimes, all we need is the willingness to learn along the way.

If I had turned down that opportunity simply because I didn't know the language, I would probably have missed one of the most important learning experiences of my career so far.

And thinking about it, perhaps that's exactly what the phrase on the wall at Goalfy is all about.

**Growth is uncomfortable. And I'm glad it is.**
